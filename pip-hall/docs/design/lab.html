Pixel Pass, built as a platformer level

The carousel is now a side-scrolling level. Badges hang from a row of blocks, a small hero walks to whoever you're looking at, and tapping a badge makes the hero jump and spin it over. A coin pops out of the block every time. The badge was rebuilt on real ID-card proportions, and the handheld got grips, a rocker and proper buttons. Every sprite here was drawn for this project.

The badge, part by part

Real ID cards are 54 × 85.6 mm. The badge uses that shape on a 4px pixel grid: 56 × 88 pixels, shown at 224 × 352. It's built in three layers like a real lanyard ID: the plastic holder, the printed card inside it, and the clear sleeve's glare on top. Stickers sit outside all three so they can overlap the edges.

  1. Lanyard, clip and swivel. Woven strap with stitch marks, a two-tone metal clip. It hangs from a block in the level, so the badge swings from a real pivot.
  2. Holder. Sage plastic, 3px thick, with a lit top-left edge and a shaded bottom-right so it reads as an object. The bottom lip carries the PIXENDO print.
  3. Slot hole. Where the clip passes through, punched out of the holder.
  4. Header band. PIXEL PASS emblem block, the wordmark, and the member number. Numbers are given in approval order.
  5. Photo window. A tiny level scene behind the member, with corner marks like a character-select screen. An uploaded photo replaces the scene; the generated sprite is the fallback.
  6. Department tag. Stamped over the window's corner so it reads at a glance.
  7. Name plate. Up to two lines in the pixel face, then the GitHub handle and role.
  8. Stats. Real counts only: projects, skills, and the year they joined.
  9. Coin line. The divider is a row of tiny coins, not a rule.
  10. Item slots. GitHub, LinkedIn and website as inventory slots. An empty slot is dashed, so a missing link looks unfilled, not broken.
  11. QR and serial. In the barcode spot. The serial (PXP·number·handle) is printed under it like a card ID.
  12. Sleeve glare. Two diagonal stripes that slide as the badge swings.
  13. Stickers. Die-cut: white rim, ink outline, a hard shadow. At most three, and their spots come from the username, so a card always looks the same.

Back of the badge (Quest Log): a three-line bio, the first three projects as numbered stages with their language, skills shown as small tags (each skill is a power-up), and VIEW PROFILE. A member with no projects gets a "No quests yet" slot instead of an empty space.

The handheld, and why it's shaped this way

It's a wide slab you'd hold with both hands, with the screen in the middle. Movement controls sit on the left grip and actions on the right, so your thumbs stay where they would on a real handheld. On a phone the grips fold into one control row under the screen. The phone already is the handheld, so the shell gets out of the way.

One pixel size everywhere

World, sprites and badge all use the same 4px pixel. Mixing pixel sizes (art drawn at 2px next to art at 4px) is what makes pixel UIs look cheap.

Light from the top left

Every solid object (shell, buttons, blocks, holder) gets a lit top-left edge and a shaded bottom-right. Flat things like paper, stickers and the card print get none.

Staircase corners

Corners are cut in 4px steps: two steps on small parts, four on the shell. No smooth rounded corners anywhere.

Buttons travel 4px

Hardware buttons sit on a 6px shadow and drop 4px when pressed, in two frames. It feels like a click, not a fade.

The LED tells the truth

Green when the app is online, dark when it's offline. Status lights only show real states.

Screws, grilles, print

Small details that cost nothing and make it read as hardware: four screws, two dot grilles, an embossed wordmark.

Motion, one move at a time

Two clocks run at once. Sprites animate on a frame clock (8 to 12 frames a second, stepped), so they feel like a real 16-bit game. Physical things like the badge swinging, the flip and the camera run on smooth springs. With reduced motion on, every row falls back to the last column.

Sprite sheet

All drawn for PIXEL PASS from text maps in code, so they're tiny and switch to night colours on their own. The hero is Pip, PIXENDO's host. It's also the face in the text window.

The Mario-era style, done our way

These are the platformer habits that make it feel like that era of games. Each one is used for a real job in the app, not as decoration.

A level you walk through

Browsing members is walking right through a world, and the camera scrolls like a side-scroller.

Blocks overhead

Every badge hangs from its own block. Bump it from below and something comes out.

Coins and a counter

Each flip earns a coin. The HUD counter is a quiet record of how many cards you've looked at.

A hero who jumps

Pip is how you act on the world. Flipping is a jump, not a button press.

Warp tube and goal flag

The tube at the start of the level leads to Explore. The flag at the end says you've met everyone.

HUD and power-ups

Coins, world and player count across the top. Skills on the back of the badge are power-ups.

The line we hold: no Mario, no other Nintendo characters, items, logos, sounds or console designs. Everything here is original and only shares the genre. That keeps the requirement and keeps the project safe to show publicly.
